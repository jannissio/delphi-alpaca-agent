# E — Short-Horizon Return Predictability, Calendar Effects (Wed 2 – Fri 4 Sep 2026) and Retail Options Behavior

Research analyst review, time-boxed. Scope: does any short-horizon or calendar evidence justify a **directional tilt** (put-side vs call-side) on a 0–2 DTE defined-risk credit spread in our exact three-session window, or is the honest answer "stay delta-neutral"?

Companion to the earlier reviews on 0DTE credit-spread economics, sizing and execution. Those findings are taken as given and are not re-derived here.

---

## Kurzfassung (Deutsch)

1. **Kein Kalendereffekt in unserem Fenster ist gross genug für einen Richtungs-Tilt.** Die grössten Punktschätzer (Turn-of-the-Month Tag +2: +0,13 %/Tag; Pre-Holiday: ~+0,10 %/Tag) liegen bei 0,10–0,16 Standardabweichungen der täglichen SPY-Schwankung (~0,8 %). Das ist Rauschen.
2. **Turn-of-the-Month (McConnell & Xu 2008):** Mi 2.9. ist TOM-Tag +2, Do 3.9. ist Tag +3. Genau diese beiden Tage sind die schwächsten des TOM-Fensters (t = 1,84 bzw. 1,21 — einzeln nicht signifikant). Neuere Evidenz (Han/Han/Tian, FRL 2024) meldet das vollständige Verschwinden des Effekts nach 2001.
3. **Pre-Holiday (Ariel 1990):** Fr 4.9. ist Vortag von Labor Day. Der Effekt ist für Grossunternehmen tot: Ko & Yang (2021) finden für S&P 500 und DJIA 1983–2019 t = 0,93 bzw. 0,64. Er lebt nur noch bei Small Caps. **Kein Tilt.**
4. **Kritischer, bisher nicht erfasster Fund: Der US-Arbeitsmarktbericht (Employment Situation, August 2026) erscheint Fr 4.9.2026 um 08:30 ET** — direkt vor unserer letzten Session (09:30–11:00 ET). Über den BLS-Kalender als Primärquelle verifiziert. Das dominiert jeden Kalendereffekt dieses Tages um Grössenordnungen.
5. **Die Overnight-Prämie ist verschwunden.** Boyarchenko/Larsen/Whelan: das 2–3-Uhr-ET-Fenster brachte 1998–2020 3,7 % p.a. (über 60 % der gesamten Close-to-Close-Rendite); seit 2021 praktisch null. Es gibt **keine Kompensation mehr für Overnight-Gap-Risiko** — ein Argument für intraday-lastige Positionen und gegen Übernacht-Carry auf kurzlaufenden Short-Optionen.
6. **Intraday-Momentum ist real, aber winzig und im falschen Fenster.** Gao et al. (2018) / Baltussen et al. (2021): die ersten 30 Minuten bzw. der Rest des Tages sagen die **letzten 30 Minuten** voraus — Trefferquote nur 55 % (61 % wenn beide Signale übereinstimmen), Erwartungswert rund 3 Basispunkte pro Tag. Wir sind vor 15:45 ET flach, also genau ausserhalb dieses Fensters. **Eine Regel „erste 30 Min stark positiv → lieber Put-Spread" ist nicht belegbar.**
7. **Short-Term Reversal auf Tagesebene ist tot** (Blitz/van der Grient/Honarvar 2023: „in den meisten Regionen vollständig verschwunden"). Kein Mean-Reversion-Tilt.
8. **Was Retail systematisch falsch macht, ist unser einziger belastbarer Edge:** Retail **kauft** kurzlaufende Optionen (Kauf:Verkauf = 7:1; 97 % der 0DTE-Trades starten als Kauf) und verliert damit im Schnitt −4,6 % pro 0DTE-Trade (Bogousslavsky & Muravyev). Bryzgalova et al. (JF 2023): die Verluste konzentrieren sich in Long-Positionen kurzlaufender Kontrakte; **wer diese Kontrakte verkauft, verdient signifikant — auch nach Transaktionskosten**.
9. **Retail bevorzugt Calls klar (69,4 % Volumenanteil).** Zusammen mit Constantinides/Czerwonko/Perrakis (2020: die dominierenden Portfolios enthalten „mostly calls and positions are overwhelmingly short", besonders bei **kurzer Laufzeit und hoher ATM-IV**) und Almeida et al. (2025, 0DTE-VRP ist Upside-getrieben) ergibt das drei unabhängige Hinweise, dass die **Call-Seite die reichere Seite ist**. Bondarenko (2014) widerspricht — allerdings mit Daten 1987–2000 und Monatslaufzeiten.
10. **Empfehlung: delta-neutral bleiben, aber die Prämie asymmetrisch einsammeln.** Kein Richtungs-Tilt aus Kalender-, Momentum- oder Reversal-Evidenz. Erlaubt ist ein **Skew-Tilt**: mehr Credit auf der Call-Seite (etwas näherer Short-Call-Delta), kompensiert durch eine kleinere oder weiter entfernte Put-Seite, so dass das Netto-Delta ungefähr null bleibt.
11. **Nicht wegen Einzelaktien-Earnings handeln.** de Silva/So/Smith (Review of Finance 2026): Retail verliert 5–9 % (10–14 % bei hoher erwarteter Ankündigungs-Volatilität) genau mit diesem Trade. Die Gegenseite verdient im Mittel — aber bei *einem* Namen ist es ein Münzwurf mit fettem Tail. AVGO (Mi nach Schluss, rund 4–5 % QQQ-Gewicht) ist ein Grund, am Donnerstag eher SPY als QQQ zu handeln.
12. **Vier-Bein-Strukturen kosten viel:** Vilkov (0DTE Trading Rules) findet für Iron Butterfly/Condor brutto SR 0,77, **netto −0,20**; Put-Ratio-Spreads brutto 1,18 / netto 0,93. Aber: die SEC-DERA-Studie zeigt, dass SPXW-0DTE-Spreads bis Juli 2023 dramatisch enger wurden (1 Tick in 64,7 % der Zeit gegen 0,5 % im Juli 2020) und geduldige Limit-Orders in rund 50 % der Fälle **null effektiven Spread** zahlen.
13. **Freitag: nicht vor etwa 09:45–10:00 ET einsteigen.** Das NFP-Ereignis ist um 09:30 bereits im Preis; die ersten Minuten sind die volatilsten des Tages. Vilkovs konditionale Regeln setzen bewusst um 10:00 ET an.
14. **Fazit in einem Satz:** Die einzige Evidenz, die in unserem Fenster stark genug ist, um Strikes zu bewegen, betrifft *welche Seite reicher gepreist ist* (Call-Seite, moderates Vertrauen) und *wann man nicht im Markt sein sollte* (letzte 30 Minuten am Verfallstag, erste 15–30 Minuten am NFP-Freitag) — **nicht die Richtung des Marktes**.

## Source cards

### 1. McConnell, J. J., & Xu, W. (2008). "Equity Returns at the Turn of the Month." *Financial Analysts Journal*, 64(2), 49–64.
- **Type:** Peer-reviewed empirical. **Venue/Year:** FAJ, 2008. **Citations:** 143 (OpenAlex).
- **Quality:** Citation-worthy: **yes**. The canonical modern TOM reference; 80-year US sample plus 35 countries.
- **Key findings (numbers):** TOM window = Day −1 (last trading day of prior month) through Day +3. CRSP value-weighted, **1987–2005**: Day −1 = 0.19 % (t = 2.99), Day **+1 = 0.25 %** (t = 3.73), Day **+2 = 0.13 %** (t = 1.84), Day **+3 = 0.08 %** (t = 1.21); four-day mean 0.15 % (t = 4.35) vs **−0.00 %** on all other days; difference 0.15 % (t = 3.78). Fraction positive 66 % (TOM) vs 58 % (other days). Full 1926–2005: 0.16 % vs 0.01 %. Equal-weighted returns are roughly double. Effect present in 31 of 35 countries; not explained by month-end volume or equity fund flows.
- **Relevance:** Wed 2 Sep 2026 = Day +2, Thu 3 Sep 2026 = Day +3 (last trading day of Aug 2026 was Mon 31 Aug). **These are precisely the two weakest days of the TOM window** and the only two individually insignificant even in the original sample.
- **Caveats:** Sample ends 2005. Day +2/+3 t-stats below 2. The economically large days (−1 and +1) are Mon 31 Aug and Tue 1 Sep — both before our window.

### 2. Han, L., Han, Y., & Tian, S. (2024). "The disappearing turn-of-month effect." *Finance Research Letters*, 70, 106461. DOI 10.1016/j.frl.2024.106461
- **Type:** Peer-reviewed note. **Venue/Year:** FRL, 2024. **Citations:** 3 (OpenAlex).
- **Quality:** Citation-worthy: **with caveats**. Existence, venue and authorship verified via OpenAlex; I could **not** retrieve the abstract or tables (ScienceDirect returns 403; OpenAlex has no abstract index for this record). The characterization below comes from a search-engine summary and must be treated as unverified.
- **Key findings (reported, unverified):** Complete disappearance of the TOM effect after roughly 2001, attributed to the post-decimalization collapse in transaction costs and improvement in liquidity.
- **Relevance:** Directly downgrades source 1 for a 2026 trading decision.

### 3. Ko, K.-C., & Yang, N.-T. (2021). "The Pre-Holiday Premium of Ariel (1990) Has Largely Become A Small-Firm Effect Out of Sample." *Critical Finance Review* replication series (editor: Ivo Welch). — replicating **Ariel, R. A. (1990), "High Stock Returns before Holidays: Existence and Evidence on Possible Causes," *Journal of Finance* 45(5), 1611–1626.**
- **Type:** Peer-reviewed replication/extension. **Venue/Year:** CFR, 2021. **Citations:** not retrieved.
- **Quality:** Citation-worthy: **yes**. An editor-supervised replication — exactly the "does it survive out of sample" evidence we need.
- **Key findings (numbers):** *Replication 1963–1982:* CRSP EW pre-holiday 0.49 % vs 0.06 % other; VW 0.37 % vs 0.03 %; t = 9.12 and 7.03; ratios 8.6× and 14.4×; fraction positive 86 % (EW) / 76 % (VW) vs 60 % / 54 %. *Extension 1983–2019:* EW pre-holiday falls to 0.37 % (t = 6.76) but **VW to 0.14 % (t = 1.92)**; VW t-statistics for 1990–2019 and 1995–2019 are **1.19 and 1.23**. Fraction positive for VW falls to 59 % (insignificant). **Untabulated large-cap indices, 1983–2019: DJIA t = 0.64, S&P 500 t = 0.93.** VW significance vanishes entirely once weekend and turn-of-year effects are controlled. The cumulative VW pre- vs non-pre-holiday difference has been flat since 1990.
- **Relevance:** Fri 4 Sep 2026 is the trading day before Labor Day. For an S&P 500 / SPY trader the pre-holiday premium is **statistically indistinguishable from zero** in the modern sample.
- **Caveats:** Ends 2019. Labor Day is one of eight pooled holidays; no single-holiday breakdown is given.

### 4. Boyarchenko, N., Larsen, L. C., & Whelan, P. — "Overnight Drift," *Review of Financial Studies* 36(9), 3502– (2023); FRBNY Staff Report 917 (Feb 2020, rev. Aug 2022). Update: **"The Disappearing Overnight Drift," Liberty Street Economics, 1 July 2026** (and SSRN 7035838).
- **Type:** Peer-reviewed article plus a first-party central-bank research-blog update. **Citations:** not retrieved (the RFS paper is widely cited).
- **Quality:** Citation-worthy: **yes**. The blog post is the authors' own out-of-sample test of their own published result, which is the strongest form of anomaly-decay evidence.
- **Key findings (numbers):** 1998–2020, E-mini S&P 500 futures: the **2:00–3:00 a.m. ET hour** (European open) returned **about 3.7 % annualized**, i.e. **more than 60 % of the contract's 5.9 % annualized close-to-close return**. Mechanism: inventory-risk reversal of end-of-day US order imbalances; asymmetric — selloffs produce robust positive overnight reversals, rallies much weaker. **Since 2021 that hour has averaged close to zero.** The standard deviation of closing relative signed volume fell from 6.5 % to 2.9 % ("a compression of more than half"), which the authors identify as the cause; volatility and liquidity are otherwise unchanged. Corroborating market evidence: NightShares' NSPY/NIWM overnight ETFs launched June 2022 and closed fourteen months later.
- **Relevance:** Answers RQ2 directly. There is **no longer a market-level overnight risk premium** compensating the gap risk of carrying a short 0–2 DTE option position through the close.
- **Caveats:** Futures, not SPY cash. The blog update is not peer-reviewed (the underlying RFS paper is).

### 5. Lou, D., Polk, C., & Skouras, S. (2019). "A tug of war: Overnight versus intraday expected returns." *Journal of Financial Economics*, 134(1), 192–213.
- **Type:** Peer-reviewed. **Citations:** 336 (OpenAlex).
- **Quality:** Citation-worthy: **yes** — but **not decisive for our question**.
- **Key findings:** Strong firm-level overnight and intraday return continuation with an offsetting cross-period reversal, persisting for years. Across **14 trading strategies**, profits are earned **either entirely overnight (reversal and several momentum variants) or entirely intraday**, typically with **opposite signs** across the two components. The smoothed overnight-minus-intraday return spread forecasts time variation in the strategy's close-to-close performance. Institutional ownership rises more with intraday than with overnight returns.
- **Relevance:** Establishes that overnight and intraday returns are economically distinct objects driven by different clienteles — the conceptual justification for treating "hold overnight" as its own risk decision rather than as 16 extra hours of the same exposure.
- **Caveats:** The results are **cross-sectional** (long-short stock portfolios), not a market-level index statement, so they do not by themselves determine whether an index credit spread should be carried overnight. Sample ends mid-2010s. No option-level implication.

### 6. Gao, L., Han, Y., Li, S. Z., & Zhou, G. (2018). "Market intraday momentum." *Journal of Financial Economics*, 129(2), 394–414.
- **Type:** Peer-reviewed. **Citations:** 222 (OpenAlex).
- **Quality:** Citation-worthy: **yes**.
- **Key findings:** SPY, 1993–2013. The **first half-hour return** (measured from the previous close) **positively predicts the last half-hour return**. Predictability is stronger on more volatile days, higher-volume days, recession days, and **major macroeconomic news release days**. The second-to-last half-hour also predicts the last half-hour.
- **Relevance:** The one short-horizon result that could plausibly generate a same-day directional rule. The news-day amplification matters because Fri 4 Sep is an NFP day.
- **Caveats:** The predicted object is the **last 30 minutes only**, not the day or the session.

### 7. Limkriangkrai, M., Chai, D., & Zheng, G. (2023). "Market intraday momentum: APAC evidence." *Pacific-Basin Finance Journal*, 80, 102086.
- **Type:** Peer-reviewed replication and extension. **Citations:** not retrieved.
- **Quality:** Citation-worthy: **yes**, specifically as an independent replication of the US result with reported effect sizes.
- **Key findings (numbers):** **US replication, 1996–2013:** first-half-hour coefficient **7.15** (significant at 1 %), **in-sample R² = 1.7 %**; second-to-last half-hour alone R² = 0.9 %; both together R² = 2.6 %. **Out-of-sample R²: 1.7 % (r1 alone), 0.5 % (second-to-last alone), 2.3 % (both)** — "magnitudes similar to those reported by Gao et al. (2018)." Outside the US the effect is much weaker: present in China and Japan, weak in South Korea, **absent in Hong Kong and Singapore**; weaker during the COVID crisis.
- **Relevance:** Confirms the US effect is real and quantifies it — an R² of roughly 2 % on a 30-minute return.
- **Caveats:** The APAC results reduce confidence in universality; the first-half-hour signal is not "everywhere."

### 8. Baltussen, G., Da, Z., Lammers, S., & Martens, M. (2021). "Hedging demand and market intraday momentum." *Journal of Financial Economics*, 142(1), 377–403.
- **Type:** Peer-reviewed. **Citations:** 97 (OpenAlex).
- **Quality:** Citation-worthy: **yes**. The strongest version of this literature: 60+ futures across four asset classes, 1974–2020, with an identified mechanism.
- **Key findings (numbers):** The **rest-of-day return (r_ROD: previous close to 30 minutes before close)** positively predicts the **last 30 minutes**, across equities, bonds, commodities and currencies. r_ROD beats Gao et al.'s first-half-hour signal (higher out-of-sample R²; when the two disagree, r_ROD generally wins). **The effect reverts over the next days.** Equity index futures 1/N portfolios, annualized, **before transaction costs**: (r_ONFH) 4.21 %, SR 1.07, **success rate 0.55**; (r_ONFH and r_ROD agreeing) 5.47 %, SR 1.60, **success 0.61**; (r_ROD) 6.86 %, SR 1.73, **success 0.55**; benchmark Always Long 0.44 %, SR 0.11, success 0.53. Bonds SR 0.81–1.62; commodities 0.82–1.42; currencies 0.87–1.03. **Mechanism:** negative gamma exposure (NGE) of option and leveraged-ETF market makers — intraday momentum is present for the index when NGE is negative and **strengthens as NGE becomes more negative**; the effect grows over time as leveraged ETFs are introduced.
- **Relevance:** Two things. (a) It quantifies the directional edge: a **55 % hit rate and about 6.9 %/yr from being in the market 30 minutes a day, i.e. roughly 2.7 bp of expected return per day**. (b) The gamma-hedging mechanism is the theoretical reason to be **flat before the closing 30 minutes on expiry day** — which is already the design rule.
- **Caveats:** **"Note that we do not consider transaction costs."** The authors state a positive net Sharpe survives only in the most liquid markets at roughly a one-tick cost. Futures, not options. The strategy is invested only 30 minutes per day.

### 9. Blitz, D., van der Grient, B., & Honarvar, I. (2023). "Reversing the Trend of Short-Term Reversal." SSRN 4575689 / Robeco Quant Research, September 2023.
- **Type:** Practitioner-academic working paper. **Citations:** not retrieved (SSRN abstract page returns 403).
- **Quality:** Citation-worthy: **with caveats** — Robeco house research with a commercial interest in the "enhanced" version they sell; the *negative* result on the classic effect is the credible part.
- **Key findings:** "The classic short-term reversal effect has steadily weakened over time, to the point of **now having vanished entirely in most regions**." It can be revived only by neutralizing the conflicting short-term industry- and factor-momentum exposures, which then roughly doubles the risk-adjusted return. The premium is attributed to temporary supply-demand imbalances and liquidity provision.
- **Relevance:** Kills the 1–2 day mean-reversion tilt (Jegadeesh 1990; Lehmann 1990). Consistent with the older literature showing reversal profits vanish net of trading costs (de Groot, Huij & Zhou) and with the structural-change explanation (decimalization, hedge funds, HFT liquidity supply).
- **Caveats:** I could not retrieve the paper's own tables, only the summary. Cross-sectional stock reversal, not index-level reversal.

### 10. Bogousslavsky, V., & Muravyev, D. (2025). "An Anatomy of Retail Option Trading." Working paper, 8 February 2025 (SSRN 4682388).
- **Type:** Working paper using trader-level proprietary data. **Citations:** 16 (OpenAlex, 2024 version).
- **Quality:** Citation-worthy: **yes, with caveats**. The first trader-level study of modern retail option trading; not yet peer-reviewed; the sample is *more* sophisticated than average retail (trading-journal users).
- **Key findings (numbers):** 5,182 traders, 2.4 M parent trades (0.9 M option trades), about **$15 bn**, 2020–2022. Option share of trades rises **23 % (early 2020) → 49 % (late 2022)**. **Option purchases dominate sales 7-to-1**; naked selling is rare (brokers restrict it). Median option maturity falls from **four days (2020) to under one day (2022)**; **0DTE = 24 % of all option trades**. Median holding period **half an hour** (mean 3.5 days). Average option trade return **−0.9 %**, versus a typical quoted bid-ask spread of **3.7 %** — so these traders do use limit orders. **0DTE trades earn about 3 % lower returns** than other option trades (raw coefficient −4.71 %, t = −10.09; −2.27 % to −2.95 % with controls and trader fixed effects); **the average 0DTE trade return is about −4.6 %**, similar for index and non-index. **97.1 % of non-index 0DTE trades start with a purchase** (84.5 % for non-0DTE). Average 0DTE trade size $1,044–1,077 versus $2,053–2,927 for non-0DTE; dollar loss per index 0DTE trade −$44.80 versus −$32.00 for non-0DTE. **Naked option sales, though rare, earn +20 % on average, robustly across specifications.** Option-only traders lose **$547 per month**. Little evidence of positive skewness in realized dollar profits (skewness −0.24 for stock trades, −0.14 for option trades among multi-day traders) — so **not** lottery-seeking in realized outcomes.
- **Relevance:** The most direct evidence on who is on the other side of a 0DTE credit spread and how they do. "+20 % on naked sales, −4.6 % on 0DTE buys" is our edge stated in one line.
- **Caveats:** Not peer-reviewed. Selected (journal-using, hence more sophisticated) retail sample. The authors' own framing is that retail losses are *smaller* than feared — do not overstate the edge. Covers 2020–2022, the widest-spread era.

### 11. Bryzgalova, S., Pavlova, A., & Sikorskaya, T. (2023). "Retail Trading in Options and the Rise of the Big Three Wholesalers." *The Journal of Finance*, 78(6), 3465–3514.
- **Type:** Peer-reviewed, top-3 finance journal. **Citations:** 120 (OpenAlex).
- **Quality:** Citation-worthy: **yes**.
- **Key findings (numbers):** Retail reached **over 60 % of total options market volume**; **about 90 % of payment for order flow goes to three wholesalers**. **49.9 % of retail (SLIM) option volume is in weeklies** versus 41.5 % for non-retail. Options with **under one week to expiry carry a 12.6 % average quoted bid-ask spread; effective spread 6.6 %** (so retail does receive price improvement, but it is still orders of magnitude above equities). **42 % of retail trades are "micro" (up to $250)**, with spreads of **28.7–29 %**. Leverage in weeklies often exceeds 50×. **Call volume share = 69.4 %** — retail strongly prefers calls to puts. **Aggregate retail loss of $2.1 bn (November 2019 – June 2021) at a 10-day horizon**; aggregate *indirect* trading costs **$6.4 bn** versus roughly $0.9 bn of direct commissions. **"The aggregate net losses are concentrated in trades in short-term contracts… there are two types of retail investors in our data: those who buy short-term options and lose money and those who sell these contracts and make significant profits, even after transaction costs."** High retail call/put volume imbalances predict next-day underlying returns, which the authors read as price pressure rather than information.
- **Relevance:** (a) Confirms that the winning side is the seller of short-dated contracts. (b) **The 69.4 % call share is a demand-pressure argument that the call wing is the richer wing** — directly relevant to asymmetric strike placement. (c) The 12.6 % quoted / 6.6 % effective spread is the cost bar patient limit orders must beat.
- **Caveats:** Single-name equity options plus ETFs; sample ends mid-2021, i.e. before the spread narrowing documented in source 13. Retail is identified via single-leg price-improvement auctions, a proxy that source 13 argues materially *understates* retail activity on the limit order book.

### 12. de Silva, T., So, E. C., & Smith, K. (2026). "Losing is optional: retail option trading and expected announcement volatility." *Review of Finance*, 30(2), 489–535. Advance access 24 October 2025. DOI 10.1093/rof/rfaf052
- **Type:** Peer-reviewed. **Citations:** 19 (OpenAlex, on the 2022 working-paper version). **Title note:** the published title is *"…and Expected Announcement Volatility,"* not "…Earnings Announcement Volatility" — the brief cites the older working-paper title.
- **Quality:** Citation-worthy: **yes**.
- **Key findings (numbers):** Retail buys options in a concentrated fashion **before earnings announcements, especially those with greater expected abnormal volatility (EAV)**, and shifts disproportionately from stocks into options as anticipated announcement volatility rises. Three wealth-depleting behaviors: overpaying relative to realized volatility, enormous bid-ask spreads, and not closing positions until **weeks after** the announcement. **Straddles on high-EAV announcements underperform those on low-EAV announcements by an average of 11 %.** Retail loses **5–9 % of option investments on average**, and **10–14 % for high-expected-volatility announcements**; the half-spread alone costs an additional **about 9 %** of the investment. Retail also loses roughly 5 % of non-earnings dollar trading volume. Underperformance continues over the **two weeks following** the announcement.
- **Relevance:** Defines precisely what NOT to do (buy premium into a known event) and identifies the profitable side (selling high-EAV premium) — while the 11 % figure is a **cross-sectional average over many announcements**, not a per-name expectation.
- **Caveats:** Single-name equity options; multi-week horizons; says nothing about 0–2 DTE index structures. Straddles are chosen with at least 13 days to expiry after the announcement.

### 13. Fu, L., Li, S., Musto, D. K., & Pearson, N. D. (2025). "Hope at a Reasonable Price: Customer Use of Limit Orders in the 0DTE Market." SEC DERA Working Paper, 16 March 2025.
- **Type:** Regulator working paper (SEC Division of Economic and Risk Analysis) with academic co-authors. **Citations:** not retrieved.
- **Quality:** Citation-worthy: **yes, with caveats** (DERA working papers are explicitly preliminary and are not Commission positions). Uses full OPRA trade-and-quote data on SPXW.
- **Key findings (numbers):** Identifies and corrects an OPRA **trade/quote sequencing error** — the quote resulting from a trade typically gets an *earlier* timestamp than the trade itself — that biases every prior 0DTE execution-cost estimate. After correction: **customers are at the BBO more than half the time** in recent data for actively traded slightly-OTM options, and a large fraction of volume is customer **non-marketable limit orders (NMLOs)** executing **at low cost**. **50 % of customer market-turning orders have a zero effective spread** relative to the midpoint at submission. The counterparty's realized spread is about **2.5 cents** one second after the trade in the early period, **half that** in the later period, and **below a penny** one minute after — "much less than even a $0.05 tick." **Spread compression:** in July 2020 the ATM SPXW BBO around 1 pm was one tick wide **just 0.5 %** of the time and three-or-more ticks wide **76 %** of the time; by **July 2023** it was one tick wide **64.7 %** and two ticks **35.3 %**, and only rarely wider. **66 % of market-turning-order trades and 68 % of passive-order trades are for a single contract**, plus 23 % and 21 % for two to five contracts — i.e. overwhelmingly retail-sized, executing on the limit order book rather than through price-improvement auctions, which contradicts the routing assumption in Beckmeyer et al. (2023) and Bryzgalova et al. (2023).
- **Relevance:** (a) **Empirically validates the patient package-limit-order execution rule for SPXW 0DTE specifically** — Muravyev & Pearson (2020) generalized to our exact instrument and tenor, by one of the same authors. (b) Materially softens the "four-leg condors are too expensive" conclusion: the 2020–2022 spread estimates underpinning much of the 0DTE cost literature are roughly twice too pessimistic for 2023 onward. (c) Implies our counterparty is largely one-contract retail.
- **Caveats:** SPXW (index, cash-settled), not SPY. Preliminary. Says nothing about the *fill probability* of a four-leg package versus two legs, which is the real reason to prefer single-sided spreads.

### 14. Vilkov, G. "0DTE Trading Rules: Tail Risk, Implementation, and Tactical Timing." SSRN 4641356 (January 2024; public replication package updated through January 2026, github.com/vilkovgr/0dte-strategies).
- **Type:** Working paper with a public AI-augmented replication package. **Citations:** not retrieved (SSRN 403).
- **Quality:** Citation-worthy: **with caveats**. Existence verified (SSRN ID, ResearchGate record, the author's own research page, and the public repo). The numbers below come from the author's annotated paper in the repository, **not** from a peer-reviewed version. Treat as indicative, not established.
- **Key findings (numbers):** Sample 09/2016–01/2026, SPXW 0DTE, entry at **10:00 ET**, held to the close. **A positive 0DTE variance risk premium exists but is economically small** — the median realized VRP from 10:00 ET to expiry is about **0.0011 % of the underlying**. Unconditional net PNL distributions are wide, state-dependent and dominated by tail risk rather than by stable mean carry; **expected shortfall at 1 % is roughly 0.58–1.58 % of the underlying**. Conditional out-of-sample results (rolling 252-day logistic model on 10:00 ET implied variance, implied skewness, slope proxies and lagged realized moments): **put ratio spreads gross SR 1.18 / net 0.93; strangles and straddles 0.56 / 0.39; iron butterfly and condor gross 0.77 / net −0.20**; top-3 equal-weight basket 1.12 / 0.82; all-strategies basket 0.81 / 0.25. Assumed costs: option-level half-spread plus a fixed **0.5 bp** slippage/fee. **Regime dependence: downside-oriented structures (bear put spreads, put ratio spreads) earn substantially higher average PNL in high-VIX states, while upside-oriented call-spread structures perform better in low-VIX states.** "Unconditional 0DTE exposure is difficult to justify as a standing allocation once one accounts for realistic execution and downside capital usage."
- **Relevance:** (a) The **iron condor gross 0.77 → net −0.20** figure is the sharpest available warning that four-leg 0DTE structures can give the entire premium back in frictions. (b) The high-VIX / low-VIX regime split is a **conditioning rule the LLM is permitted to apply** (regime classification, not ticker or size selection). (c) The 10:00 ET entry convention corroborates avoiding the opening half-hour.
- **Caveats:** Not peer-reviewed; author-supplied numbers from a self-replication repository; SPXW; single author. Do not treat these Sharpe ratios as facts.

### 15. Bondarenko, O. (2014). "Why Are Put Options So Expensive?" *The Quarterly Journal of Finance*, 4(3), 1450015. (Working paper 2003, SSRN 375784.)
- **Type:** Peer-reviewed. **Citations:** 135 (QJF version) plus 142 (SSRN version), OpenAlex.
- **Quality:** Citation-worthy: **yes**, but the sample is old.
- **Key findings (numbers):** S&P 500 **futures** options, **August 1987 – December 2000**, roughly one-month holding periods. **Average excess return of −39 % per month for ATM puts** and **−95 % per month for OTM puts at moneyness k = 0.94**; negative and highly significant at every moneyness. **"For calls, AR is generally positive but not statistically significant."** Model-free test: no model within a broad class (arbitrary preferences, possibly incorrect beliefs, Peso-problem-robust) can rationalize the put returns; to do so, a crash of the October 1987 magnitude would have to occur **1.3 times per year**. Risk-adjusted "gamma" ranges from 4.3 (deep ITM puts) to **131 (deep OTM puts)**. The Sharpe ratio for *selling* puts exceeds the market's at every moneyness, substantially so for ATM and OTM strikes.
- **Relevance:** The canonical "put side is rich" citation, and the **main piece of evidence pointing the opposite way** from the modern call-side findings (sources 11 and 16, plus Almeida et al. 2025). It must be reported as a genuine contradiction.
- **Caveats:** **Sample ends in 2000** — before decimalization, the modern VIX complex, weeklies and 0DTE. Monthly maturity. Dominated by the post-1987 crash-fear regime. The "calls not significantly mispriced" result is a statement about *one-month* calls in the 1990s, not about 0DTE calls in 2026.

### 16. Constantinides, G. M., Czerwonko, M., & Perrakis, S. (2020). "Mispriced index option portfolios." *Financial Management*, 49(2), 297–330. (NBER Working Paper 23708, August 2017.)
- **Type:** Peer-reviewed. **Citations:** 26 (OpenAlex). **Author note:** the brief lists "Constantinides, Jackwerth & Savov"; the correct author list for this paper is Constantinides, Czerwonko & Perrakis. (Jackwerth is a co-author on the related 2011 out-of-sample paper.)
- **Quality:** Citation-worthy: **yes**. The cleanest available test of "does index option mispricing survive frictions," because options are conservatively **bought at the ask and written at the bid**.
- **Key findings (numbers):** 1990–2013, S&P 500 index options at **28, 14 and 7 days to maturity**, with a 0.25 % one-way transaction cost on the index. The optimal portfolio of a utility-maximizing investor in the index plus cash **becomes stochastically dominated when overlaid with a zero-net-cost option portfolio in most months**. Formal stochastic-dominance tests **"resoundingly reject" the null of non-dominance for the 28-, 14- and 7-day portfolios under every portfolio-selection criterion**, weakest for 28-day options (rejected only at 5 % with 5 % right-tail trimming). **"The portfolios include mostly calls and positions are overwhelmingly short."** Dominance is prevalent when **ATM implied volatility is high**, when **right skew is low**, and when **option maturity is short**; the paper reports "a gradual increase in short call positions as the maturity" shortens. Results replicate on CAC and DAX index options. Not explained by priced factors or by a non-monotonic stochastic discount factor. The earlier, less flexible approach (Constantinides, Czerwonko, Jackwerth & Perrakis 2011) found dominance in only one-third to one-half of months; the flexible-portfolio approach finds it almost everywhere.
- **Relevance:** Answers RQ5's "does mispricing survive frictions" with **yes** — and answers our tilt question in an unexpected way: the frictions-robust mispricing lives on the **short call** side, and is **strongest at the shortest maturity and when ATM-IV is high**. That is a peer-reviewed, cost-inclusive argument for weighting the call wing on a short-dated structure.
- **Caveats:** Ends 2013, so no 0DTE. The authors partly interpret "overwhelmingly short calls" as consistent with covered-call writing practice. Stochastic dominance is a statement about a *utility-improving overlay*, not a standalone tradeable Sharpe ratio. The 0.25 % index transaction cost is conservative for 2026.

### 17. Dew-Becker, I., Giglio, S., Le, A., & Rodriguez, M. (2017). "The price of variance risk." *Journal of Financial Economics*, 123(2), 225–250.
- **Type:** Peer-reviewed. **Citations:** 191 (OpenAlex).
- **Quality:** Citation-worthy: **yes**.
- **Key findings (numbers):** 1996–2014, variance swaps and forwards from one month to 14 years. **"Between 1996 and 2014, it was costless on average to hedge news about future variance at horizons ranging from 1 quarter to 14 years. Only unexpected, transitory realized variance was significantly priced."** The **one-month variance claim has an annualized Sharpe ratio of −1.3** (so a short position earns about +1.3); it is **more negative by 0.9 than the two-month claim** (p = 0.03) and **at least 1.3 lower than every horizon beyond that** (p < 0.01). The estimated Sharpe ratio is **actually positive at every maturity beyond three months**. The slope of the variance term structure **falls from 3.7 annualized percentage points at the one-month maturity to an insignificant 0.3 points at three months**. For scale, the authors note the aggregate stock market's annual Sharpe has historically been about 0.3. The correlation between average returns and skewness across maturities is −0.92.
- **Relevance:** Answers RQ5's "where along the maturity curve does the premium sit": **entirely at the very short end**. This is the strongest available justification for trading 0–2 DTE rather than 30–45 DTE.
- **Caveats:** **The shortest maturity studied is one month.** The paper says nothing about 0–2 days, where microstructure and dealer gamma dominate. Extrapolating the −1.3 Sharpe to 0DTE is plausible but not demonstrated — and Vilkov (source 14) shows what happens to it after 0DTE execution costs. These are gross, frictionless variance-swap returns.

## Answers to research questions 1–6

### RQ1 — Calendar effects in our exact window

**Turn of the month.** Our Wednesday and Thursday are TOM Days +2 and +3. Under the standard McConnell–Xu (2008) definition the TOM window runs from Day −1 through Day +3, so both days are inside the window — but they are the *tail* of it. In the 1987–2005 CRSP value-weighted sample the mean daily returns are +0.13 % (t = 1.84) and +0.08 % (t = 1.21), versus +0.19 % and +0.25 % on Days −1 and +1 (t = 2.99 and 3.73). The two economically meaningful days had already passed before our account started trading. Even taking the point estimates at face value, +0.13 % is **0.16 standard deviations** of a daily SPY move and +0.08 % is **0.10 σ**. Han, Han & Tian (2024, *Finance Research Letters*) report the effect disappearing entirely after 2001, which I could not verify but which is consistent with the general anomaly-decay literature (McLean & Pontiff 2016) and with the mechanism they propose (transaction-cost collapse). **Verdict: not economically meaningful, not actionable.**

**Pre-holiday.** Friday 4 September is the trading day before Labor Day. Ariel's (1990) original result was enormous: pre-holiday returns 9 to 14 times the non-pre-holiday mean, over a third of the market's total 1963–1982 return earned on eight days a year. Ko & Yang (2021) replicate that exactly and then extend it: **for large-cap indices it is gone.** DJIA and S&P 500 1983–2019 give t-statistics of 0.64 and 0.93. The CRSP value-weighted premium falls to 0.14 % (t = 1.92) for 1983–2019 and to t = 1.19 / 1.23 for 1990–2019 and 1995–2019, and loses significance entirely once weekend and turn-of-year effects are controlled. The fraction of positive pre-holiday days for the VW index falls from 76 % to 59 %. What survives is a small-firm effect (EW−VW = +0.07 %, t = 2.32). **We trade SPY/QQQ, so the effect does not apply to us. Verdict: not actionable.**

**September effect / day-of-week.** Both are dead for our purposes. Day-of-week effects were clear in the 1980s and faded through the 1990s; recent US ETF studies find no significant calendar anomaly in the muted months (March, May, June, August, September, October) in either 2000–2009 or 2010–2021. Three days of a "weak month" is a meaningless sample in any case. **Not actionable.**

**Macro cycle — the one thing that does matter.** The brief's event list omitted a tier-1 event. I verified from the **BLS September 2026 release calendar** that the **Employment Situation for August 2026 is released Friday 4 September 2026 at 08:30 ET** — 60 minutes before the open of our final session. Thursday carries only revised Q2 Productivity & Costs (second tier) plus weekly claims. Wednesday has no BLS release. This single scheduled event is larger than every calendar effect in this section combined, and it changes the design of the Friday session (see Design implications D5).

**Bottom line on RQ1: no calendar effect in our window is large enough relative to ~0.8 %/day noise to justify moving a strike. The largest surviving point estimate is 0.16 σ and it is statistically insignificant even in the paper that made it famous.**

### RQ2 — Overnight versus intraday, and what it implies for holding short options overnight

The market-level fact that mattered is gone. Boyarchenko, Larsen & Whelan documented that between 1998 and 2020 the single hour from **2:00 to 3:00 a.m. ET** produced about **3.7 % annualized — over 60 % of the E-mini's entire 5.9 % annualized close-to-close return**. Their July 2026 update reports that this hour has **averaged close to zero since 2021**, and identifies the cause: the dispersion of end-of-day order imbalances, which drove the inventory-risk reversal, collapsed from a 6.5 % to a 2.9 % standard deviation. The market's own verdict was quick — NightShares launched overnight ETFs in June 2022 on the original result and closed them fourteen months later.

Lou, Polk & Skouras (2019) supply the conceptual frame rather than the number: across 14 strategies, profits accrue **entirely overnight or entirely intraday, usually with opposite signs**, because different clienteles trade in the two sessions. Overnight is not "more of the same exposure."

**Implication for us, stated plainly.** Carrying a short 0–2 DTE option position through the close means accepting gap risk — the tail that turns a defined-risk spread into a max-loss spread in one print — in exchange for an overnight equity risk premium that **no longer exists**. A short put spread carried overnight is short the gap; a short call spread carried overnight is short the (historically positive, now zero) overnight drift. Either way there is no longer a directional premium being harvested to pay for the risk. The only thing being harvested overnight is theta, and theta on a 0DTE position that expires the next day is precisely what the gap can destroy.

**Recommendation: strongly prefer same-session, open-and-close positions.** If a position must be carried overnight (e.g. a 1DTE opened Wednesday for Thursday), it should be (a) fully defined-risk with wings actually purchased, (b) sized so that a full max-loss overnight gap is within the 2 %-per-session budget, and (c) avoided entirely on Wednesday night, because AVGO/SNOW/HPE report after Wednesday's close and AVGO alone is a 4–5 % QQQ constituent. Note also that this is the strongest evidence-backed reason to **prefer 0DTE over 1DTE for a three-day competition**: the overnight leg of the 1DTE trade is uncompensated risk.

### RQ3 — Intraday momentum and reversal: could "first 30 min strongly positive → prefer put credit spreads" be justified?

**No. The evidence is real, well-replicated, and points at a window we do not trade, with an effect size two orders of magnitude below the noise.**

The effect: Gao, Han, Li & Zhou (2018, *JFE*) show that SPY's first-half-hour return predicts its **last-half-hour** return, 1993–2013. Limkriangkrai, Chai & Zheng (2023) replicate the US result independently: coefficient 7.15 (1 % significance), **in-sample R² = 1.7 %**, **out-of-sample R² = 1.7 %**, rising to 2.6 %/2.3 % when the second-to-last half-hour is added. Baltussen, Da, Lammers & Martens (2021, *JFE*) generalize it to 60+ futures over 1974–2020, show that the whole **rest-of-day** return is a better predictor than the first half-hour, and identify the mechanism: **negative dealer gamma from options and leveraged ETFs**, verified with a direct NGE proxy and with the timing of LETF introductions.

Why it does not help us:

1. **Wrong window.** Every version of the effect predicts the **last 30 minutes** (15:30–16:00 ET). Our design is flat before 15:45 ET on expiry day, and Friday's session ends at 11:00 ET. There is no overlap.
2. **Wrong magnitude.** Baltussen's equity-index timing strategy earns **6.86 % annualized while being in the market only 30 minutes a day** — about **2.7 basis points of expected return per day**, i.e. **0.03 σ**. To move a 12-delta short strike you need a shift measured in tenths of a percent, not hundredths.
3. **Wrong hit rate for a directional bet.** The success rate is **0.55** (0.61 when the first-half-hour and rest-of-day signals agree, which happens on a subset of days). A 55 % coin flip on a defined-risk spread whose loss leg is 3–5× the credit is not a positive-expectation directional trade.
4. **It mean-reverts.** Baltussen et al. explicitly report the effect "reverts over the next days" — so it is a within-day liquidity/hedging phenomenon, not a forecast of tomorrow.
5. **It is untested net of costs.** "Note that we do not consider transaction costs." The authors state a positive net Sharpe survives only in the most liquid futures at roughly one tick of cost. Options at 0DTE are not that market.
6. **The proposed rule has the wrong sign anyway.** "First 30 min strongly positive → prefer put credit spreads" is a *momentum* rule (expect continued strength, so sell puts). The literature supports momentum only into the close, and the strongest predictor is the *whole day so far*, not the first half hour. Applying it at 10:00 ET, before eleven of the day's thirteen half-hours have happened, uses the weakest version of the signal at the point of maximum remaining variance.

**Short-term reversal at daily horizons** (Jegadeesh 1990; Lehmann 1990) offers no alternative. Blitz, van der Grient & Honarvar (2023) state that the classic effect has "steadily weakened over time, to the point of now having vanished entirely in most regions"; Cheng, Hameed, Subrahmanyam & Titman (2017) show the decline (1.84 % → 1.25 % risk-adjusted contrarian profit); de Groot, Huij & Zhou show it does not survive trading costs. It is also cross-sectional, not index-level.

**The one legitimate use of this literature is defensive, and it is already in the design.** Baltussen's gamma mechanism says the final 30 minutes on a day with large dealer short gamma is when price is most likely to trend violently in whichever direction it was already going. That is exactly the wrong time to hold a short 0DTE spread near the money. **Being flat before 15:45 ET is the correct, literature-supported rule and should be kept.**

### RQ4 — PEAD and retail earnings behavior: what retail does wrong, and what it means for us

**PEAD itself is irrelevant to us.** Bernard & Thomas (1989) found roughly a 2 % drift over **60 trading days** after an earnings surprise (about 4 % quarterly, 18 % annualized). Our entire holding period is hours. Moreover the effect has declined materially in recent decades (Chordia et al. 2014; Martineau 2019). A 60-day drift cannot be harvested with a 0–2 DTE spread, and no drift signal survives the LLM-must-not-pick-tickers constraint.

**Retail earnings behavior, by contrast, is the most useful finding in this review.** Three independent datasets agree:

- **de Silva, So & Smith (2026, *Review of Finance*):** retail buys options concentrated **before** earnings, and preferentially before announcements with **greater expected abnormal volatility**. They then exhibit "a trio of wealth-depleting behaviors": overpaying relative to realized volatility, incurring enormous bid-ask spreads (the half-spread alone costs **about 9 % of the investment**), and **not closing positions until weeks after** the announcement. Net: **−5 % to −9 % on average, −10 % to −14 % for high-expected-volatility announcements.** ATM straddles on high-EAV announcements underperform low-EAV straddles by **11 %**.
- **Bryzgalova, Pavlova & Sikorskaya (2023, *JF*):** retail is over 60 % of options volume; **49.9 % of retail volume is in weeklies**, where the average quoted spread is **12.6 %** (6.6 % effective); **42 % of retail trades are under $250** with spreads near **29 %**; aggregate retail loss **$2.1 bn** over Nov 2019 – Jun 2021, of which **$6.4 bn** is indirect trading cost versus $0.9 bn of commissions. Crucially: **"the aggregate net losses are concentrated in trades in short-term contracts… those who buy short-term options lose money and those who sell these contracts make significant profits, even after transaction costs."**
- **Bogousslavsky & Muravyev (2025):** trader-level. **Option purchases dominate sales 7-to-1.** **97.1 % of non-index 0DTE trades open with a purchase.** Median option maturity fell from four days to under one day between 2020 and 2022; **0DTE is 24 % of all option trades**; median holding period is **half an hour**. Average option trade earns **−0.9 %**; **0DTE trades earn about −4.6 %**, underperforming other option trades by 4.71 % raw and 2.27–2.95 % with controls. And the mirror image: **naked option sales earn +20 % on average, robust across specifications.**

**What this implies for us at 0–2 DTE.** We are the seller. The population on the other side is buying short-dated premium, in small size, at wide spreads, and holding for minutes to hours. That is the population the literature says loses. Three caveats keep this honest:

1. Bogousslavsky & Muravyev's own headline is that retail losses are **smaller than feared** (−0.9 % overall, well inside a 3.7 % quoted spread) — retail does use limit orders. The Fu/Li/Musto/Pearson SEC paper reaches the same conclusion for SPXW 0DTE specifically: **50 % of customer market-turning orders pay a zero effective spread**, and the counterparty's realized spread is under a penny a minute after the trade. **The retail edge is a real but modest edge, not a windfall.** Anyone sizing on the assumption of a fat, easy premium is misreading the literature.
2. The losses are concentrated in **long** positions in short-term contracts. Being short those contracts is the correct side, but the *structure* determines whether we keep the premium — Vilkov's iron condor result (gross SR 0.77 → **net −0.20**) shows how much a four-leg structure can hand back.
3. **The retail-side flow is skewed to calls: 69.4 % of retail option volume is calls** (Bryzgalova et al.). If retail demand pressure is what makes short-dated options rich, it makes the *call* wing rich. See RQ6.

**And what it means for the AVGO / ZS / LULU / DOCU earnings in our window: do not trade them.** The profitable side of a high-EAV earnings event is selling premium — but the 11 % and 10–14 % figures are cross-sectional averages across hundreds of announcements. On a *single* name, over *one* announcement, with a fresh $100k account and a risk-averse mandate, a short structure over AVGO earnings is a coin flip with a fat left tail and no diversification. The literature's advice here is "do not be the buyer," not "be the seller of one name." If any earnings adjustment is made, it should be **defensive**: prefer SPY over QQQ on Thursday because AVGO's ~4–5 % QQQ weight makes the Thursday QQQ open a partly-mechanical gap.

### RQ5 — Citation chasing

**Vilkov, "0DTE Trading Rules" (SSRN 4641356) — EXISTS, verified.** Full title in the replication package: "0DTE Trading Rules: Tail Risk, Implementation, and Tactical Timing," Grigory Vilkov, dated 14 January 2024, with a public replication repo (github.com/vilkovgr/0dte-strategies) updated through January 2026. Sample 09/2016–01/2026, SPXW, 10:00 ET entry to close. Key numbers: a positive 0DTE variance risk premium exists but the **median realized VRP from 10:00 ET to expiry is about 0.0011 % of the underlying**; **1 % expected shortfall is 0.58–1.58 % of the underlying**. Conditional out-of-sample Sharpe ratios (gross / net of half-spread + 0.5 bp): **put ratio spread 1.18 / 0.93; strangle & straddle 0.56 / 0.39; iron butterfly & condor 0.77 / −0.20**; top-3 basket 1.12 / 0.82; all-strategies basket 0.81 / 0.25. Regime split: **downside-oriented structures do better in high-VIX states, upside/call-spread structures in low-VIX states.** Author's conclusion: "unconditional 0DTE exposure is difficult to justify as a standing allocation once one accounts for realistic execution and downside capital usage." **Caveat: not peer-reviewed; numbers are from the author's own replication package.**

**Bondarenko, "Why Are Put Options So Expensive?" — EXISTS, published.** *Quarterly Journal of Finance* 4(3), 2014, article 1450015; working paper 2003 (SSRN 375784). 135 + 142 citations (OpenAlex). S&P 500 futures options, **August 1987 – December 2000**: average excess return **−39 % per month for ATM puts**, **−95 % per month for OTM puts at k = 0.94**, significant at every moneyness; **calls generally positive but not statistically significant**. To rationalize these returns, an October-1987-sized crash would have to occur **1.3 times per year**. Risk-adjusted "gamma" 4.3 (deep ITM puts) to **131 (deep OTM puts)**. Selling puts beats the market's Sharpe at every strike. **Caveat: the sample ends in 2000, at one-month maturity — it says nothing about 0DTE, and its "calls are fine" finding is the main evidence against a call-side tilt.**

**Dew-Becker, Giglio, Le & Rodriguez, "The Price of Variance Risk" — EXISTS.** *JFE* 123(2), 2017, 225–250; 191 citations. (The brief cites "Dew-Becker, Giglio & Kelly"; the correct co-authors are Le and Rodriguez.) **Where the premium sits: entirely at the front end.** The **one-month variance claim has an annualized Sharpe of −1.3** (short earns +1.3); the Sharpe is **at least 1.3 lower at one month than at every maturity beyond three months**, and is **actually positive beyond three months**. The variance-swap slope **falls from 3.7 annualized percentage points at one month to an insignificant 0.3 points at three months**. Their summary: "it was costless on average to hedge news about future variance at horizons from 1 quarter to 14 years. Only unexpected, transitory realized variance was significantly priced." **This is the best available theoretical justification for our 0–2 DTE tenor choice — with the important caveat that their shortest maturity is one month, and extrapolation to 0DTE is not demonstrated.**

**Cboe CNDR — index exists, methodology retrieved, returns only partially verified.** Definition (Cboe primary): sell monthly ~20-delta OTM SPX put and call, buy ~5-delta OTM put and call as wings, T-bill collateral, AM settlement, monthly roll. Verified from Cboe's own "Benchmark Indices Series" post: **maximum drawdown CNDR 19 % vs BFLY 47.1 % vs S&P 500 51 %**; CNDR monthly returns land between 0 % and 2 % about **59 %** of the time; in the 11 months since 1986 when the S&P moved more than 10 %, neither CNDR nor BFLY moved more than 10 %. **Not verified against a Cboe primary document** (secondary sources only): CAGR **+9.11 % Jan 1987 – Jan 2010**, **≈ −0.72 % annualized Jan 2010 – 2024**, full 37-year CAGR **5.28 %**. **Honest statement of the wing give-up: wings cut the maximum drawdown roughly in three (19 % vs 51 %) and, on the secondary figures, consumed essentially the entire strategy return over 2010–2024.** Note the CNDR short strikes are 20-delta and monthly — far more aggressive than our 10–16-delta 0–2 DTE structure — so it is an imperfect analogue.

**Constantinides, Czerwonko & Perrakis, "Mispriced Index Option Portfolios" — EXISTS, published.** *Financial Management* 49(2), 2020, 297–330 (NBER WP 23708). 26 citations. (The brief cites "Constantinides, Jackwerth & Savov"; the correct author list is Constantinides, Czerwonko & Perrakis. Jackwerth co-authored the related 2011 out-of-sample paper.) **Does mispricing survive frictions? Yes.** With options **bought at the ask and written at the bid** and a 0.25 % one-way index transaction cost, the utility-maximizing index-plus-cash portfolio becomes **stochastically dominated** when overlaid with a zero-net-cost option portfolio in most months of 1990–2013. Dominance is rejected "resoundingly" at **28, 14 and 7 days to maturity** under every selection criterion, **weakest at 28 days**. The dominating portfolios contain **"mostly calls and positions are overwhelmingly short,"** with **short call positions increasing as maturity shortens**. Dominance is **prevalent when ATM implied volatility is high and when right skew is low**. Replicated on CAC and DAX. **This is the single most decision-relevant citation-chase result in this review** (see RQ6 and D2).

### RQ6 — Synthesis: should the agent tilt direction at all?

**No directional tilt. Yes to one skew tilt and two timing rules.**

**The case against a directional tilt is overwhelming and can be stated in one paragraph.** Every candidate signal in our window — turn of the month days +2/+3, the pre-Labor-Day effect, the September effect, day-of-week, daily short-term reversal, and intraday momentum — produces a point estimate between **0 and 0.16 standard deviations** of a daily SPY move, and every one of them is either statistically insignificant in the original paper, documented to have decayed out of sample, or confined to a 30-minute window we do not trade. Meanwhile the daily noise is 0.8 % and a 0–2 DTE credit spread's payoff is convex in exactly that noise: the loss leg is typically 3–5× the credit. A directional tilt with a 55 % hit rate — the *best* number in this literature, from Baltussen's futures timing strategy — loses money on a 1:4 payoff structure. **The signal-to-payoff arithmetic, not just the significance tests, rules out a directional bet.**

**However, "delta-neutral" and "symmetric" are not the same thing, and the distinction is where the actual finding lies.** Three sources, using three different methods and datasets, converge on the claim that **at short maturities the call wing is the richer wing**:

- **Constantinides, Czerwonko & Perrakis (2020)** — peer-reviewed, cost-inclusive, 1990–2013: the frictions-robust dominating option overlays consist of **"mostly calls," "overwhelmingly short,"** with the short-call weight **increasing as maturity shortens** and dominance strongest at their 7-day tenor and when **ATM-IV is high**.
- **Bryzgalova, Pavlova & Sikorskaya (2023)** — *Journal of Finance*, order-flow: **69.4 % of retail option volume is calls**, and retail is now the majority of options volume. If retail demand pressure is what makes short-dated options rich, it is disproportionately making *calls* rich.
- **Almeida, Freire & Hizmeri (2025)** — per the brief: the 0DTE variance risk premium is **upside-driven**, i.e. the call side carries it.

Against this stands **Bondarenko (2014)**, the canonical "puts are too expensive" paper — but with data ending in **2000**, at one-month maturity, in the post-1987 crash-fear regime, and with the explicit finding that *calls* were not significantly mispriced *then*. The two bodies of evidence are not really in conflict; they describe different eras and tenors. The pre-2003 world had institutional put demand and no retail call flow; the post-2020 world has a retail call bid on top of the put skew. **Confidence in "call wing is richer at 0–2 DTE": medium.** It is enough to shade strike placement. It is not enough to run a naked one-sided call spread.

**The synthesis, therefore:** stay **delta-neutral at the position level**, but collect the premium **asymmetrically** — take more credit and a slightly closer short strike on the call side, and offset the resulting short delta with a smaller or farther-out put spread so the net delta is approximately zero. This is exactly the "asymmetric iron condor" already in the core design, and this review supplies the reason to make the asymmetry **call-heavy rather than put-heavy** — with one explicit regime override from Vilkov: in a **high-VIX state, downside-oriented structures do better and upside/call-spread structures do worse**, so the asymmetry should flatten or invert when VIX is elevated. That regime classification is precisely the kind of judgment the LLM is permitted to make.

**Two timing rules survive with high confidence, both defensive:**
1. **Flat before 15:45 ET on expiry day** — supported by the gamma-hedging mechanism in Baltussen et al. (2021), which says the final 30 minutes is when dealer short gamma most reliably amplifies whatever move is underway.
2. **Do not open Friday's position before roughly 09:45–10:00 ET** — the jobs report at 08:30 ET means the first minutes carry the widest spreads and the highest realized volatility of the day, and Vilkov's entire rule set is calibrated to a 10:00 ET entry.

## Design implications

Ranked, each with sources, effect size versus the ~0.8 %/day noise, and confidence.

### D1. **Do not take a directional tilt from any calendar or short-horizon signal.** (Confidence: **high**)
Sources: McConnell & Xu 2008; Han, Han & Tian 2024; Ko & Yang 2021; Blitz et al. 2023; Gao et al. 2018; Baltussen et al. 2021. Combined effect size of every applicable calendar signal across all three sessions: **≤ 0.3 % of cumulative drift against ~1.4 % of cumulative noise**, with no component individually significant post-2000. **Not strong enough to move a strike.** Net delta target per session: approximately zero.

### D2. **Make the asymmetry call-heavy, not put-heavy — but keep net delta ≈ 0.** (Confidence: **medium**)
Sources: Constantinides, Czerwonko & Perrakis 2020 ("mostly calls… overwhelmingly short," strongest at short maturity and high ATM-IV); Bryzgalova et al. 2023 (69.4 % retail call volume); Almeida, Freire & Hizmeri 2025 (0DTE VRP upside-driven). **Contradicted by** Bondarenko 2014 (1987–2000, one-month tenor). Concrete form: within the 10–16-delta band, place the **short call nearer 16 delta and the short put nearer 10–12 delta**, then size the two verticals so the net position delta is roughly flat. Effect size: this is a claim about **relative richness of the two wings**, not about direction, so it does not need to beat the 0.8 % noise — it needs only to be a better-than-coin-flip ranking of which side is overpriced, which three independent sources support. **This is strong enough to affect strike placement; it is not strong enough to justify a single-sided call spread with unhedged short delta.**

### D3. **Override D2 toward neutrality or the put side when VIX is elevated.** (Confidence: **low-medium**)
Source: Vilkov, 0DTE Trading Rules (downside-oriented structures earn substantially more in high-VIX states; call-spread structures better in low-VIX states). Also consistent with Constantinides et al., who find dominance strongest when ATM-IV is high — meaning the *whole* structure is richer, not just one wing. **This is a legitimate LLM task**: classify the volatility regime (e.g. VIX terciles or a simple threshold versus the trailing 60-day distribution) and flatten the call-side asymmetry when in the top tercile. **Single non-peer-reviewed source — treat as a tie-breaker, not a driver.**

### D4. **Prefer same-session (0DTE) positions; treat any overnight carry as uncompensated.** (Confidence: **medium-high**)
Sources: Boyarchenko, Larsen & Whelan 2026 update (the overnight drift, formerly 3.7 %/yr in a single hour and over 60 % of the index's total return, is ~0 since 2021); Lou, Polk & Skouras 2019 (overnight and intraday are distinct exposures). Effect size: the removal of a **~3.7 %/yr** premium that used to justify overnight risk. Practical rule: **do not carry a short 0DTE/1DTE position through Wednesday's close** (AVGO, SNOW and HPE all report after that close; AVGO is ~4–5 % of QQQ). If a 1DTE is opened Wednesday, size it so that a full max-loss gap sits inside the 2 %-per-session budget.

### D5. **Friday: do not enter before ~09:45–10:00 ET; the 08:30 ET jobs report has already happened.** (Confidence: **high** on the fact, **medium** on the rule)
Source: BLS September 2026 release calendar (primary, retrieved and verified). Two consequences. **(a) Positive:** by 09:45 the event is in the price, implied volatility typically compresses after the release, and the 09:30–11:00 window is the most liquid of the day — a reasonable window to sell short-dated premium. **(b) Negative:** post-NFP trend days are real, the first 15–30 minutes carry the day's widest spreads, and Gao et al. (2018) specifically find intraday predictability is stronger on **major macroeconomic news release days**, which is a statement about elevated within-day trending, not about a tradeable edge for us. **Waiting until 09:45–10:00 costs 15–30 minutes of a 90-minute session and removes the worst execution window.** Vilkov's entire conditional rule set uses a 10:00 ET entry.

### D6. **Stay flat before 15:45 ET on expiry day — keep this rule, it is now mechanistically justified.** (Confidence: **high**)
Sources: Baltussen, Da, Lammers & Martens 2021 (dealer negative gamma is the identified cause of last-30-minute momentum; the effect strengthens as dealer gamma becomes more negative); Gao et al. 2018. The literature's own mechanism says the closing 30 minutes is when a short-gamma position is most likely to be run over. **This was already the design; this review upgrades it from prudence to mechanism.**

### D7. **Prefer SPY over QQQ on Thursday.** (Confidence: **medium**)
Rationale: AVGO reports Wednesday after the close and is roughly 4–5 % of QQQ, so an ±8 % AVGO gap mechanically moves QQQ ±0.3–0.4 % at Thursday's open before any market-wide news. SPY's AVGO weight is roughly four times smaller. Effect size: **0.4–0.5 σ of added, one-sided, non-diversifiable open gap risk on QQQ** — larger than every calendar effect in this report combined. If QQQ is traded Thursday, widen the strikes.

### D8. **Do not trade single-name earnings.** (Confidence: **high**)
Sources: de Silva, So & Smith 2026; Bogousslavsky & Muravyev 2025; Bryzgalova et al. 2023. The literature identifies the *losing* side (buying premium into a high-expected-volatility announcement: −10 % to −14 %) and the *winning* side (selling it, +11 % relative on average). But the winning number is a **cross-sectional average over hundreds of announcements**. One name, one announcement, a fresh $100k account and a risk-averse mandate is the worst possible setting for harvesting an average. The correct use of this literature is **not to be the buyer**, plus D7.

### D9. **Keep patient package limit orders; the 2023+ execution environment is much better than the 2020–2022 literature suggests.** (Confidence: **medium-high**)
Sources: Fu, Li, Musto & Pearson 2025 (SEC DERA) — 50 % of customer market-turning orders achieve a **zero effective spread**; counterparty realized spread is under a penny a minute after the trade; the ATM SPXW BBO was one tick wide **0.5 % of the time in July 2020 versus 64.7 % in July 2023**. This means (a) the patient-limit-order rule is validated on our exact instrument class and tenor, and (b) the four-leg-condor cost penalty inherited from 2020–2022 data is roughly **twice too pessimistic** for today. It does **not** mean condors are free — Vilkov's condor still goes from gross SR 0.77 to net −0.20, and the unaddressed risk with four legs is **fill probability**, not just spread.

### D10. **State the wing give-up honestly in the writeup.** (Confidence: **low-medium** on the numbers)
Source: Cboe CNDR. Wings reduce maximum drawdown from about 51 % (S&P 500) to about 19 % (CNDR), i.e. roughly a two-thirds cut in tail loss; on unverified secondary figures they also consumed essentially the entire strategy return over 2010–2024 (CAGR ≈ −0.72 %) after producing +9.11 % over 1987–2010. For a risk-averse mandate over three sessions, **buying the wings is correct anyway** — the objective is survival, not CAGR — but the return cost should be stated rather than glossed.

### What the retail-behavior literature says NOT to do

Every one of these is a documented, quantified retail error. The agent must not commit them:

1. **Do not buy short-dated premium.** Retail 0DTE buys average **−4.6 %** per trade (Bogousslavsky & Muravyev). We are always the seller.
2. **Do not buy premium into a known volatility event.** −5 % to −9 % on average, **−10 % to −14 %** for high expected announcement volatility (de Silva et al.).
3. **Do not cross the spread.** Retail's indirect trading costs were **$6.4 bn versus $0.9 bn of commissions** (Bryzgalova et al.); the sub-$250 trades pay **~29 %** spreads. Half-spread alone costs about **9 %** of an earnings option investment. Patient limit orders get zero effective spread half the time (SEC DERA).
4. **Do not trade micro size in wide-spread contracts.** 42 % of retail trades are ≤$250 and pay the worst spreads. Fixed costs and spread dominate at that size.
5. **Do not hold past the thesis.** Retail keeps earnings positions for **weeks** after the event (de Silva et al.). Our positions must have a hard time-based exit, not just a price-based one.
6. **Do not confuse "options are lottery tickets" with "sellers get a lottery premium."** Bogousslavsky & Muravyev find **negative** skewness in realized retail dollar profits (−0.14 for options) — the lottery framing is not borne out in realized outcomes, so the seller should not expect a skewness premium either. Expect small, noisy carry with a fat left tail (Vilkov: 1 % ES of 0.58–1.58 % of the underlying).
7. **Do not scale up on early wins.** Beckmeyer/Branger/Gayda's 0DTE iron condor distribution (per the brief: mean −1.1 %, median **+5.5 %**, P25 −24 %, P5 −100 % of margin) is the canonical trap — the median trade wins, so a two-day sample will very likely show early profits that say nothing about expectation.
